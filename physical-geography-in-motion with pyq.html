Unit II · The Earth · Chapter 2

The Origin and Evolution of the Earth

From a tiny ball of energy to a blue planet with air, oceans and life

In short

  • The universe began with the Big Bang about 13.7 billion years ago and is still expanding.
  • Gas clouds (nebulae) gave rise to galaxies and stars; around stars, dust and gas built planetesimals, then planets.
  • The young Earth (4,600 million years ago) was hot and barren. It sorted itself into layers by differentiation.
  • Air came from degassing of the interior, oceans from condensed rain, and oxygen from photosynthesis. Life began about 3,800 million years ago.
01

Early theories

How the Earth and planets formed

The German philosopher Immanuel Kant suggested an early idea, and the mathematician Laplace revised it in 1796. This is the Nebular Hypothesis: planets formed from a cloud of material around a young, slowly rotating sun.

In 1950, Otto Schmidt (Russia) and Carl Weizsäcker (Germany) revised it. They said the sun was surrounded by a solar nebula of mostly hydrogen, helium and dust. Friction and collisions made a disc-shaped cloud, and planets formed by accretion (sticking together). Later, scientists turned to the bigger question of how the whole universe began.

02

The Big Bang

Also called the expanding universe hypothesis

In 1920 Edwin Hubble showed that galaxies are moving further apart: the universe is expanding. Picture dots on a balloon moving apart as you blow it up. The analogy is only partly right: on a balloon the dots also grow, but real galaxies do not expand. Only the space between them increases.

STAGE 1

Singular atom

All matter was in one "tiny ball" with unimaginably small volume, infinite temperature and infinite density.

STAGE 2

The bang

About 13.7 billion years ago it exploded. Some energy turned into matter. Expansion was fastest in the first fractions of a second, then slowed. First atoms formed within 3 minutes.

STAGE 3

Transparent universe

Within 300,000 years the temperature fell to 4,500 K, atomic matter formed and the universe became transparent.

ALTERNATIVE

Steady state

Hoyle's idea that the universe stays roughly the same at any time. Evidence for expansion now favours the Big Bang.

Stars and galaxies

Matter and energy were unevenly spread in the early universe. These density differences created differences in gravity, which pulled matter together into galaxies. A galaxy begins as a huge cloud of hydrogen called a nebula. Clumps inside it grow denser and become stars, about 5–6 billion years ago. A galaxy is 80,000–150,000 light years across.

A light year measures distance, not time. Light travels 300,000 km per second, so in a year it covers 9.461 × 10¹² km. The sun is 149,598,000 km away, which light crosses in about 8.3 minutes.
03

Formation of planets

Three stages
STAGE 1

Core and disc

Stars are lumps of gas in a nebula. Gravity forms a core, with a huge rotating disc of gas and dust around it.

STAGE 2

Planetesimals

The cloud condenses into small rounded objects that stick together (cohesion) and collide, forming many small bodies called planetesimals.

STAGE 3

Planets

The many planetesimals accrete into a few large bodies: the planets.

04

Evolution of the Earth

4,600 million years ago to today

At first the Earth was barren, rocky and hot, with a thin atmosphere of hydrogen and helium. Three big changes turned it into the planet we know: layers formed inside, an atmosphere and oceans developed, and life appeared.

Lithosphere: differentiation

The early Earth was mostly in a volatile (molten) state. As density increased, the temperature inside rose and materials separated by density: heavy ones like iron sank to the centre and light ones rose. The giant impact that formed the moon heated it further. As it cooled it shrank and a crust formed. This sorting is called differentiation, and it produced the crust, mantle, outer core and inner core, with density increasing towards the core.

Atmosphere and hydrosphere

  1. Stage 1

    Loss of the primordial atmosphere. Solar winds stripped away the early hydrogen and helium, on Earth and on all terrestrial planets.

  2. Stage 2

    Degassing. As the Earth cooled, the hot interior released gases and water vapour through volcanoes: mostly water vapour, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and ammonia, with very little free oxygen.

  3. Oceans · within 500 million years

    Water vapour condensed; rain dissolved carbon dioxide and cooled the Earth further, causing more rain. Water collected in depressions as oceans, which are about 4,000 million years old.

  4. Stage 3 · photosynthesis

    Life began about 3,800 million years ago and stayed in the oceans for a long time. Photosynthesis evolved 2,500–3,000 million years ago, filling the oceans with oxygen. About 2,000 million years ago oxygen began to flood the atmosphere.

Origin of life

Scientists see the origin of life as a chemical reaction that first made complex organic molecules and then assembled them so they could copy themselves, turning non-living matter into living matter. Fossils record past life. Microscopic structures like present-day blue algae are found in rocks older than about 3,000 million years.

Exercise answers

2 (i)What is meant by the process of differentiation?

The separation of the Earth's materials by density. As the young Earth heated up, heavy materials such as iron sank towards the centre and lighter ones moved up, forming layers: crust, mantle, outer core and inner core.

2 (ii)What was the nature of the Earth's surface initially?

Barren, rocky and hot, with only a thin atmosphere of hydrogen and helium and no oceans or life.

2 (iii)Which gases initially formed the Earth's atmosphere?

The first atmosphere was hydrogen and helium, later stripped by solar winds. The next atmosphere, from degassing, was mostly water vapour, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and ammonia, with very little free oxygen.

3 (i)Write an explanatory note on the Big Bang theory.

The Big Bang theory, or expanding universe hypothesis, is the most accepted explanation of the universe's origin. All matter was once concentrated in a tiny "singular atom" of infinitely small volume, infinite temperature and density. About 13.7 billion years ago it exploded and expanded rapidly; some energy turned into matter. The first atoms formed within three minutes. Within 300,000 years the temperature fell to 4,500 K, atomic matter formed and the universe became transparent. Uneven distribution of matter led to gravitational clumping into galaxies, and nebulae within galaxies formed stars about 5–6 billion years ago. Hubble's evidence (1920) that galaxies are moving apart supports the theory: the space between galaxies increases, though the galaxies themselves do not expand. Hoyle's steady state concept was an alternative, but evidence now favours the expanding universe.

3 (ii)List the stages in the evolution of the Earth and explain each briefly.

(1) Evolution of the lithosphere: the hot, volatile Earth separated by density (differentiation); iron sank and lighter materials rose, forming crust, mantle and core; the moon-forming impact added heat. (2) Evolution of the atmosphere in three stages: the primordial hydrogen–helium atmosphere was stripped by solar winds; degassing from the interior and volcanoes supplied water vapour, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and ammonia; and photosynthesis later added oxygen, which flooded the atmosphere about 2,000 million years ago. (3) Evolution of the hydrosphere: condensing water vapour fell as rain and filled depressions, forming oceans within 500 million years of the Earth's birth. (4) Origin of life: about 3,800 million years ago, chemical reactions produced self-copying organic molecules; life stayed in the oceans for a long time, and fossils such as blue-algae-like structures over 3,000 million years old record it.

Unit II · The Earth · Chapter 3

Interior of the Earth

How we know what lies 6,378 km beneath our feet, and what comes out of it

In short

  • No one can reach the centre, so we use direct sources (mines, drilling, volcanoes) and indirect ones (temperature–pressure–density trends, meteors, gravity, magnetism and, most of all, earthquake waves).
  • P-waves pass through solids, liquids and gases; S-waves pass only through solids. The S-wave shadow zone proves the outer core is liquid.
  • Layers: crust (5 km under oceans, 30 km under continents), mantle to 2,900 km, liquid outer core, solid inner core of nickel and iron.
  • Magma that erupts builds volcanoes; magma that cools inside the crust forms intrusive bodies: batholiths, laccoliths, lapoliths, phacoliths, sills and dykes.
01

Sources of information

Earth's radius is about 6,378 km

Direct sources

Surface rock and mining (South African gold mines reach 3–4 km; deeper is too hot). Deep drilling: "Deep Ocean Drilling Project" and "Integrated Ocean Drilling Project"; the deepest drill, at Kola in the Arctic, reached 12 km. Volcanic eruptions bring magma to the surface for lab study, though its depth of origin is unknown.

Indirect sources

Temperature, pressure and density rise with depth; knowing their rates, scientists estimate values deep inside. Meteors are made of material similar to Earth's. Gravity is greater at the poles than the equator; differences from expected values (gravity anomalies) show how mass is distributed. Magnetic surveys map magnetic materials. Seismic activity is the most important source.

02

Earthquakes

Shaking of the Earth caused by sudden release of energy

A fault is a sharp break in crustal rocks. Rocks on either side try to move in opposite directions, but friction locks them. When the stress finally overcomes friction, the blocks slide past each other suddenly and energy is released as waves in all directions. All natural earthquakes happen in the lithosphere (up to about 200 km deep).

Focus (hypocentre)

The point inside the Earth where the energy is released.

Epicentre

The point on the surface directly above the focus, the first to feel the waves.

Seismograph

Instrument that records the waves reaching the surface.

Earthquake waves

Body waves start at the focus and travel through the body of the Earth. When they meet surface rocks they create surface waves, which travel along the surface. Waves travel faster through denser material, and they reflect (bounce back) or refract (change direction) at boundaries between materials.

The shadow zone

Some places on the surface receive no waves from a given earthquake. Seismographs within 105° of the epicentre record both P and S waves. Beyond 145° only P-waves arrive. Between 105° and 145° neither arrives: this band is the P-wave shadow zone. S-waves never arrive anywhere beyond 105°, so their shadow zone is much larger, a little over 40% of the Earth's surface. S-waves cannot cross the liquid outer core.

Types of earthquakes

Tectonic: most common, sliding along a fault. Volcanic: a special tectonic type in active volcanic areas. Collapse: roofs of underground mines cave in. Explosion: chemical or nuclear blasts. Reservoir induced: near large reservoirs.

Measuring earthquakes

Richter scale measures magnitude, the energy released (0–10). Mercalli scale measures intensity, the visible damage (1–12). Magnitude 8+ quakes are rare (once in 1–2 years); tiny ones happen almost every minute.

Effects of earthquakes. Ground shaking, differential ground settlement, land and mud slides, soil liquefaction, ground lurching, avalanches, ground displacement, floods from dam and levee failures, fires, structural collapse, falling objects and tsunamis. The first six affect landforms. A tsunami is a wave caused by a quake under the ocean, not an earthquake itself. Effects are devastating above magnitude 5.

04

Volcanoes and volcanic landforms

A volcano is a place where gases, ash and molten rock escape to the ground. It is active if it erupts now or did recently. Molten rock is called magma while underground and lava once it moves up towards or reaches the surface. Eruptions release lava flows, pyroclastic debris, volcanic bombs, ash, dust and gases (nitrogen and sulphur compounds, a little chlorine, hydrogen and argon).

Intrusive forms

Lava cooling on the surface forms volcanic rocks; magma cooling inside the crust forms plutonic rocks. The shapes it takes inside the crust are intrusive forms.

Batholith

Large dome of magma cooled deep in the crust; granite. The cooled magma chamber. Seen only when erosion removes the rock above.

Laccolith

Large dome-shaped body with a level base, fed by a pipe from below. Granite domes of the Karnataka plateau are examples.

Lapolith

Saucer-shaped body, concave to the sky.

Phacolith

Wavy mass at the base of a syncline or top of an anticline, connected to a magma chamber below.

Sill and sheet

Near-horizontal bodies along weak planes. Thick ones are sills; thin ones are sheets.

Dyke

Wall-like body that solidified almost vertically in a crack. Common in western Maharashtra; the feeders of the Deccan Traps.

Exercise answers

2 (i)What are body waves?

Waves generated by the release of energy at the focus that travel in all directions through the body of the Earth. They are of two kinds: P-waves and S-waves.

2 (ii)Name the direct sources of information about the interior.

Surface rocks and rocks from mines, deep drilling projects (such as the Deep Ocean Drilling Project and the 12 km Kola borehole), and magma brought out by volcanic eruptions.

2 (iii)Why do earthquake waves develop a shadow zone?

Waves bend (refract) as they pass through layers of different density, and S-waves cannot travel through the liquid outer core. So P-waves miss the band 105°–145° from the epicentre, and S-waves miss everything beyond 105°.

2 (iv)Explain indirect sources other than seismic activity.

Rates of increase of temperature, pressure and density with depth let scientists estimate conditions deep inside. Meteors are made of material similar to Earth's. Gravity anomalies show how mass is distributed in the crust, and magnetic surveys show the distribution of magnetic materials.

3 (i)What are the effects of earthquake waves on the rock mass they travel through?

Waves make the rocks vibrate. P-waves vibrate parallel to the direction of travel; they push on the material, creating density differences that stretch and squeeze it. S-waves vibrate perpendicular to the direction of travel in the vertical plane, creating crests and troughs in the material. Surface waves move along the surface and are the most destructive: they displace rocks and make structures collapse. Because wave speed changes with density, waves also reflect and refract at boundaries, which changes their direction. At the surface these vibrations show up as ground shaking, settlement, landslides, liquefaction, lurching, avalanches and ground displacement.

3 (ii)What are intrusive forms? Describe them.

Intrusive forms are shapes taken by magma that cools inside the crust rather than on the surface. Batholiths are large granite domes formed from cooled magma chambers deep in the crust, exposed only after erosion. Laccoliths are dome-shaped bodies with a level base fed by a pipe from below, like the granite domes of Karnataka. Lapoliths are saucer-shaped, concave to the sky. Phacoliths are wavy masses at the base of synclines or top of anticlines in folded country, connected to magma chambers. Sills (thick) and sheets (thin) are near-horizontal bodies along weak planes. Dykes are vertical, wall-like bodies formed in cracks; they are common in western Maharashtra and fed the Deccan Traps.

Unit II · The Earth · Chapter 4

Distribution of Oceans and Continents

Why the map has changed, and is still changing

In short

  • Wegener (1912): all continents were one supercontinent, Pangaea, in one ocean, Panthalassa. It split about 200 million years ago into Laurasia (north) and Gondwanaland (south).
  • His evidence was good (jig-saw fit, matching rocks, tillite, placers, fossils), but his forces were too weak.
  • Hess (1961): new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and is consumed at trenches: sea floor spreading.
  • Plate tectonics (1967): the lithosphere is broken into 7 major and some minor plates moving over the asthenosphere. Plates, not just continents, move; mantle convection drives them.
01

Continental drift

Alfred Wegener, German meteorologist, 1912

Continents cover 29% of the Earth's surface. The idea that the Americas, Europe and Africa were once joined is old: the Dutch map maker Abraham Ortelius proposed it first in 1596, and Antonio Pellegrini drew a map with the three continents together. Wegener turned it into a full theory.

Evidence for drift

Jig-saw fit

The facing coasts of Africa and South America match. Bullard (1964) used a computer to fit them at the 1,000-fathom line, not the shoreline, and the fit was almost perfect.

Rocks of the same age

A belt of 2,000-million-year-old rocks on the Brazil coast matches rocks in western Africa. The earliest marine deposits on both coasts are Jurassic, so the ocean did not exist before then.

Tillite

Sedimentary rock from glacial deposits. India's Gondwana sediments, with thick tillite at the base, have counterparts in Africa, the Falkland Islands, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia: proof of shared glaciation and drift.

Placer deposits

Rich gold placers on the Ghana coast have no source rock nearby. The gold-bearing veins are in Brazil, so the deposits came from the Brazil plateau when the two continents lay side by side.

Fossils

Mesosaurus, a small reptile of shallow brackish water, is found only in the southern Cape of South Africa and the Iraver formations of Brazil, now 4,800 km apart. Lemurs in India, Madagascar and Africa suggested a land link once called "Lemuria".

WEAK POINT

Force for drifting

Wegener proposed the pole-fleeing force (from Earth's rotation and equatorial bulge) and the tidal force (pull of the moon and sun). Most scientists found these totally inadequate.

02

Post-drift studies

New evidence from the ocean floor after World War II

Convection current theory

Arthur Holmes (1930s) proposed convection currents in the mantle, driven by heat differences from radioactive elements. This offered the force Wegener lacked.

Mapping the ocean floor

The ocean floor is full of relief: submerged mountain ranges and deep trenches near continental margins. Mid-ocean ridges are the most volcanically active. Ocean-floor rocks are much younger than continental rocks, and rocks equidistant from a ridge crest match in age and composition.

03

Sea floor spreading

Harry Hess, 1961
FACT 1

Ridges erupt

Volcanic eruptions along mid-ocean ridges bring huge amounts of lava to the surface.

FACT 2

Mirror-image rocks

Rocks equidistant on both sides of the crest match in age, chemistry and magnetism. Rocks near the ridge have normal polarity and are youngest; age increases away from the crest.

FACT 3

Young ocean crust

No ocean crust is older than 200 million years; some continental rocks are 3,200 million years old.

FACT 4

Thin sediments

Ocean-floor sediments are surprisingly thin, nowhere older than 200 million years.

FACT 5

Earthquake depth

Trenches have deep-seated earthquakes; ridges have shallow ones.

Hess reasoned that if one ocean spreads without another shrinking, crust must be consumed somewhere: it sinks back at the oceanic trenches.

04

Plate tectonics

McKenzie and Parker, and Morgan, independently in 1967

A tectonic (lithospheric) plate is a massive, irregular slab of solid rock, usually of both continental and oceanic lithosphere, moving horizontally over the asthenosphere as a rigid unit. The lithosphere is 5–100 km thick under oceans and about 200 km under continents. A plate is called continental or oceanic depending on which part is larger: the Pacific plate is oceanic; the Eurasian plate is continental.

Seven major plates

Antarctica and surrounding ocean · North American · South American · Pacific · India–Australia–New Zealand · Africa with eastern Atlantic floor · Eurasia and adjacent ocean.

Important minor plates

Cocos: Central America–Pacific · Nazca: South America–Pacific · Arabian: mostly Saudi Arabia · Philippine: Asiatic–Pacific · Caroline: Philippine–Indian, north of New Guinea.

Three kinds of plate boundary

CRUST CREATED

Divergent

Plates pull apart at spreading sites and new crust forms. Best example: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, separating the American plates from the Eurasian and African plates.

CRUST DESTROYED

Convergent

One plate dives under another at a subduction zone. Three types: oceanic–continental, oceanic–oceanic and continental–continental.

NEITHER

Transform

Plates slide past each other horizontally. Transform faults are generally perpendicular to mid-ocean ridges, because eruptions do not happen along the whole crest at once and Earth's rotation affects the separated blocks.

SPEED

Rates of movement

Measured from magnetic stripes. Slowest: Arctic Ridge, under 2.5 cm/yr. Fastest: East Pacific Rise near Easter Island, about 3,400 km west of Chile, over 15 cm/yr.

What drives the plates? Heat from radioactive decay and residual heat makes the softened mantle beneath the plates move in circles: hot material rises, spreads, cools and sinks again. This convection cell, first suggested by Holmes, influenced Hess and is the driving force of plate movement.

Exercise answers

2 (i)What forces did Wegener suggest for the movement of continents?

The pole-fleeing force, related to the Earth's rotation and its equatorial bulge, and the tidal force, caused by the attraction of the moon and the sun. Most scholars found them inadequate.

2 (ii)How are convection currents in the mantle initiated and maintained?

They begin with heat differences in the mantle caused by radioactive decay (and residual heat). Hot material rises, spreads, cools and sinks back, and this cycle repeats continuously as a convection cell.

2 (iii)Difference between transform and convergent or divergent boundaries?

At transform boundaries crust is neither created nor destroyed; plates simply slide past each other. Divergent boundaries create new crust, and convergent boundaries destroy crust by subduction.

2 (iv)Where was the Indian landmass during the formation of the Deccan Traps?

Close to the equator. The lava outpouring began about 60 million years ago while India was moving north towards Asia, still separated from it by the Tethys Sea.

3 (i)What is the evidence for continental drift?

(1) Jig-saw fit: the coasts of Africa and South America match, confirmed by Bullard's 1964 computer fit at the 1,000-fathom line. (2) Rocks of the same age: 2,000-million-year-old rocks in Brazil match those of western Africa; the earliest marine deposits on both coasts are Jurassic, so the ocean is younger. (3) Tillite: glacial sediments of the Gondwana system in India have counterparts in Africa, the Falklands, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia, showing shared glaciation. (4) Placer deposits: gold on the Ghana coast came from source veins in Brazil, when the continents were joined. (5) Fossils: Mesosaurus occurs only in South Africa and Brazil, 4,800 km apart across an ocean; lemurs occur in India, Madagascar and Africa.

3 (ii)Basic difference between drift theory and plate tectonics.

Drift theory says continents alone moved, splitting from one supercontinent, Pangaea, about 200 million years ago, driven by pole-fleeing and tidal forces that proved inadequate; its evidence came mostly from land. Plate tectonics says the whole lithosphere is divided into seven major and several minor rigid plates of continental and oceanic lithosphere that move over the asthenosphere. What moves is the plate, not the continent alone. Movement is driven by mantle convection powered by radioactive and residual heat. Continents have wandered throughout geological time, and Pangaea itself formed by the convergence of earlier landmasses. Plate tectonics also explains boundaries (divergent, convergent, transform) and the pattern of earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains.

3 (iii)Which post-drift discoveries revived interest in the distribution of oceans and continents?

Holmes' convection current theory (1930s) supplied a possible driving force. Post-war ocean-floor mapping revealed mid-ocean ridges with active volcanoes and deep trenches near continental margins. Dating showed ocean crust is much younger than continental crust, nowhere older than 200 million years, and that rocks equidistant from ridge crests match in age, composition and magnetism, with stripes of normal and reverse polarity. Ocean sediments were thin. Earthquakes were shallow at ridges and deep at trenches. These facts led to Hess's sea floor spreading (1961) and then to plate tectonics (1967).

Unit III · Landforms · Chapter 5

Geomorphic Processes

Two opposing teams shape the Earth's surface: one builds it up, the other wears it down

In short

  • Endogenic forces from inside the Earth (diastrophism, volcanism) build land up. Exogenic forces powered by the sun (weathering, mass wasting, erosion, deposition) wear it down. Relief remains because both keep working.
  • Weathering breaks rock in place: chemical, physical and biological.
  • Mass movements are gravity moving debris downhill without any agent: creep, flow, slide, fall.
  • Erosion needs an agent (water, ice, wind, waves, groundwater). Soil forms from weathered material through parent material, topography, climate, biological activity and time.
01

Endogenic and exogenic forces

Why the Earth's surface is uneven

Exogenic forces, driven by sunlight and the atmosphere, cause degradation (wearing down of high land) and aggradation (filling up of low land). Wearing down of relief by erosion is called gradation. Endogenic forces, from heat inside the Earth, keep raising parts of the crust, so exogenic processes never manage to make the surface flat.

A geomorphic process is any endogenic or exogenic force that stresses earth materials physically or chemically and changes the surface. A geomorphic agent is a mobile medium (running water, ice, wind, waves and currents, groundwater) that removes, transports and deposits material. Gravity powers every downslope movement; without gravity and gradients there would be no erosion, transport or deposition.

Endogenic processes

Energy from radioactivity, rotational and tidal friction and primordial heat. Diastrophism moves and raises the crust: orogeny (mountain building by severe folding in long narrow belts), epeirogeny (uplift or warping of large parts, continent building), earthquakes (local minor movements) and plate tectonics (horizontal movement). These cause faulting and pressure–volume–temperature changes that metamorphose rocks. Volcanism moves magma onto or towards the surface.

Exogenic processes

Energy from the sun through the atmosphere, plus slopes created by tectonics. Gravity and other stresses (force per unit area) deform materials; shear stress breaks rocks. Temperature changes, crystallisation and melting add molecular stress, and chemical processes loosen grains. Together they are called denudation ("to strip off"): weathering, mass wasting, erosion and transportation. Their effect depends on climate (temperature and precipitation), vegetation, and rock type and structure.

02

Weathering

An in-situ (on-site) process: rock breaks where it lies

Weathering is the mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks by elements of weather and climate. Very little material moves. Climate controls both the kind of weathering and the depth of the weathered mantle. Usually all three groups act together, with one dominant.

CHEMICAL

Decompose and dissolve

Solution, carbonation, hydration, oxidation and reduction, by oxygen, water and acids. Water, air (oxygen, carbon dioxide) and heat speed them up; decaying organisms add carbon dioxide underground.

PHYSICAL

Break apart

Applied forces: gravity (overburden, load, shear), expansion (temperature change, crystal growth, animals) and water pressure from wetting and drying. Mostly thermal expansion and pressure release, repeated until rock fatigues.

BIOLOGICAL

Living things

Earthworms, termites and rodents burrow and expose fresh surfaces; humans plough. Decaying matter makes humic and carbonic acids. Plant roots exert tremendous pressure that splits rock.

SPECIAL EFFECT

Exfoliation

A result, not a process: curved sheets flake off, leaving smooth rounded surfaces. Exfoliation domes come from unloading; tors from thermal expansion.

Why weathering matters. It prepares the way for soils, erosion and mass movements. Forests and biodiversity depend on the depth of the weathering mantle. It also causes enrichment: leaching removes some material and concentrates valuable ores of iron, manganese, aluminium and copper.
03

Mass movements

Debris moving downslope under the direct pull of gravity

In mass movements, air, water or ice do not carry the debris; the debris may carry them. No agent is involved, so mass movements are not erosion. Weathering helps but is not required. Movements range from slow to rapid and include creep, flow, slide and fall. The three forms of movement are heave, flow and slide.

Favouring conditions: weak unconsolidated materials, thinly bedded rocks, faults, steeply dipping beds, vertical cliffs and steep slopes, heavy or torrential rain, and little vegetation.

Slump

One or more units of debris slip with a backward rotation relative to the slope.

Debris slide

Rapid rolling or sliding of earth debris without backward rotation.

Debris fall

Nearly free fall of debris from a vertical or overhanging face.

Rockslide

Individual rock masses slide down bedding, joint or fault surfaces; very fast and destructive on steep slopes.

Rock fall

Blocks fall freely, away from the slope. Affects only surface layers, unlike a rockslide which affects material to depth.

Activating causes

Removal of support from below · steeper or higher slopes · overloading by added material or by heavy rain and saturation · removal of load from slopes · earthquakes, explosions or machinery · excessive natural seepage · heavy drawdown of water from lakes, reservoirs and rivers · indiscriminate removal of vegetation.

India: two hill regions

Himalayas: tectonically active, mostly sedimentary and loose deposits, very steep, so landslides and debris avalanches are frequent. Nilgiris and Western Ghats: stable hard rock, yet they still occur because of near-vertical cliffs, strong temperature-driven mechanical weathering and very heavy rain in short periods, so rock falls are common.

04

Erosion and deposition

Erosion is the acquisition and transportation of rock debris by agents: running water, groundwater, glaciers, wind and waves. Abrasion by the debris they carry helps. Weathering aids erosion but is not a precondition. Weathering, mass wasting and erosion are all degradational, but erosion causes most of the continuous change on the surface.

Deposition follows erosion. On gentler slopes agents lose speed and energy, and coarse material settles first, fine material later. Deposition is not really the work of any agent; the same agents act as aggradational agents.

Soil formation

Pedogenesis · pedology is soil science

Soil is a dynamic medium of constant chemical, physical and biological activity: a result of decay and a medium for growth. Formation depends first on weathering, whose mantle is the basic input.

Five soil-forming factors

PASSIVE

Parent material

Weathered rock in place (residual soils) or transported deposits (transported soils). Texture, structure and mineral composition matter. Young soils and limestone soils show strong links with the parent rock.

PASSIVE

Topography

Controls sunlight exposure and drainage. Soils are thin on steep slopes, thick on flat uplands, best on gentle slopes; flat areas may develop thick dark clay rich in organic matter.

ACTIVE

Climate

Moisture and temperature. Excess water carries components down (eluviation) and deposits them below (illuviation). Wet equatorial climates remove silica (desilication). In dry climates capillary rise leaves salts as hardpans. Tropical areas with intermediate rain form calcium carbonate nodules (kanker). Warmth speeds chemical activity (except carbonation), so tropical soils are deep and tundra soils mostly broken rock.

ACTIVE

Biological activity

Adds organic matter, moisture and nitrogen. Dead plants give humus. Cold climates accumulate humus and peat (slow bacteria); humid tropics have little humus (fast bacteria). Nitrogen fixation: Rhizobium in root nodules of legumes. Ants, termites, earthworms and rodents rework the soil.

PASSIVE

Time

Decides maturity and profile development. Soils on fresh alluvium or glacial till are young, with no or poor horizons. No fixed time can be given.

Exercise answers

2 (i)Weathering is responsible for biodiversity on Earth. How?

Weathering produces the weathering mantle from which soils form. Vegetation (forests) depends on the depth of this mantle, and biomes and biodiversity are basically a result of vegetation.

2 (ii)Which mass movements are rapid and perceptible? List them.

Landslides of relatively dry material: slump, debris slide, debris fall, rockslide and rock fall. Debris avalanches are also rapid.

2 (iii)What are the mobile exogenic agents and their prime job?

Running water, groundwater, glaciers, wind, and waves and currents. Their prime job is denudation: eroding (removing) and transporting earth materials, then depositing them at lower levels.

2 (iv)Is weathering essential for soil formation? Why?

Yes. Soil formation depends first on weathering; the weathered mantle is the basic input that organisms then colonise and turn into soil. Weathering alone is not enough, though: the other factors are also needed.

3 (i)"Our earth is a playfield for two opposing groups of geomorphic processes." Discuss.

Endogenic processes, powered by internal heat from radioactivity, rotational and tidal friction and primordial heat, build up the crust: diastrophism (orogeny, epeirogeny, earthquakes, plate movements) raises and deforms land, and volcanism brings magma up. Exogenic processes, powered ultimately by the sun, wear the land down: weathering breaks rock, mass movements and erosion by water, ice, wind and waves remove it (degradation), and deposition fills basins (aggradation). Because endogenic forces keep uplifting parts of the surface while exogenic forces keep lowering them, the surface never becomes level. Variations in heat flow, crustal strength, climate and rock type make both groups act unevenly, so relief persists as long as the two opposing groups remain active.

3 (ii)Exogenic processes derive their ultimate energy from the sun. Explain.

The sun's heat sets up thermal gradients in the atmosphere that create winds and drive evaporation and precipitation, which feed running water, glaciers and groundwater. Winds generate waves and currents. Daily and seasonal temperature changes, freezing and thawing cause physical weathering, and warmth speeds chemical and biological weathering. Climatic regions with their own exogenic processes exist because insolation varies with latitude, season and land–water distribution. Gravity and tectonic slopes provide direction, but the energy that mobilises the agents comes from solar heating.

3 (iii)Are physical and chemical weathering independent of each other? Explain.

No. They rarely act alone. Physical breaking (expansion, pressure release, wedging by roots or burrowing) increases surface area and opens cracks, letting water, air and acids enter and speeding chemical reactions. Chemical weathering (solution, hydration, oxidation) loosens bonds between grains and weakens rock, making it easier to break physically; hydration also makes minerals swell. Biological activity links both: roots split rocks mechanically while decaying matter adds humic and carbonic acids. One process usually dominates in a climate, but each helps the other.

3 (iv)Distinguish soil formation from soil-forming factors. What is the role of climate and biological activity?

Soil formation (pedogenesis) is the sequence of events: weathered material is colonised by bacteria, lichens and mosses, humus accumulates, grasses, bushes and trees grow, roots and burrowing animals make it porous, and a mature soil forms. Soil-forming factors are the controls that decide how this happens: parent material, topography, climate, biological activity and time. Climate is active: moisture drives chemical and biological activity and moves components down (eluviation, illuviation), causes desilication in wet tropics and hardpans in dry areas, and forms kanker; temperature speeds or slows reactions, giving deep tropical soils and shallow tundra soils. Biological activity adds organic matter, moisture and nitrogen; humus forms from dead plants, organic acids decompose minerals, bacteria fix nitrogen, and animals mix the soil. Bacterial speed explains peat in cold regions and low humus in the humid tropics.

Unit III · Landforms · Chapter 6

Landforms and their Evolution

How running water, groundwater, glaciers, waves and wind carve and build the land

In short

  • A landform is a small to medium parcel of the surface; related landforms make a landscape. Landforms evolve through youth, mature and old stages.
  • Running water (humid regions): V-valleys, gorges, canyons, potholes, terraces; fans, deltas, floodplains, levees, meanders and ox-bow lakes.
  • Groundwater (limestone): karst features such as sinkholes, uvalas, lapies, caves, stalactites and stalagmites.
  • Glaciers: cirques, horns, arêtes, U-troughs, moraines, eskers, drumlins. Waves: cliffs, stacks, bars, spits, lagoons. Wind: pediments, playas, mushroom rocks and dunes.
00

The basic ideas

SMALL TO MEDIUM

Landform

A small to medium tract of the earth's surface. Each has its own shape, size and material, and is made by particular geomorphic processes and agents.

LARGE

Landscape

Several related landforms together. A landscape is a large tract of the earth's surface.

CHANGE OVER TIME

Evolution

Landforms change slowly or quickly as processes keep acting. Climate change and vertical or horizontal earth movements can change how strongly processes act, or replace them.

LIKE A LIFE CYCLE

Stages

A landmass passes through stages comparable to human life: youth, mature and old age.

Two aspects of evolution. (1) A part of the earth's surface changes from one landform into another. (2) An individual landform changes after it has formed. So every landform has a beginning and a history of development.
01

Running water

Most important agent in humid regions with heavy rainfall

Running water has two parts. Overland flow moves as a sheet over the general land surface. Linear flow moves as streams and rivers in valleys. Steep, youthful rivers mostly erode. As continued erosion makes the channel gentler, the river loses speed and deposits more. The gentler the slope, the greater the deposition.

Overland flow causes sheet erosion. Water collects along irregularities into small, narrow rills, which grow into long, wide gullies, which deepen, widen, lengthen and join into a network of valleys. Early on, down-cutting dominates and removes waterfalls and cascades. Later, lateral erosion of valley sides takes over, slopes and divides are lowered, and finally a lowland of faint relief called a peneplain ("almost plain") remains, with a few resistant remnants called monadnocks.

Erosional landforms

Valleys

Begin as rills, grow into gullies, then valleys. Types include the V-shaped valley, gorge and canyon. The valley type depends on the type and structure of the rock.

Gorge

Deep valley with very steep, almost straight sides. About as wide at the top as at the bottom. Forms in hard rock. Example: Kaveri near Hogenakkal, Tamil Nadu.

Canyon

Steep, step-like side slopes; can be as deep as a gorge but wider at the top than the bottom. A variant of the gorge, common in horizontally bedded sedimentary rock. Example: Colorado river, USA.

Incised (entrenched) meanders

Very deep, wide meanders cut into hard rock. Steep streams normally erode their beds, but these keep a winding course while cutting down.

River terraces

Surfaces marking old valley-floor or floodplain levels. Bedrock or alluvial. Formed by erosion as the river cuts down into its own floodplain. Terraces at the same height on both sides are paired terraces.

Potholes and plunge pools

On rocky beds of hill streams, a small hollow traps pebbles and boulders. Flowing water spins them, they grind the hollow larger (abrasion), and joined potholes deepen the valley. At the foot of a waterfall the falling water and rotating boulders drill a large, deep hole called a plunge pool.

Depositional landforms

Natural levees

Low, linear, parallel ridges of coarse deposits along the banks of large rivers, often broken into separate mounds. Built when floodwater spills over the bank.

Point bars (meander bars)

Sediment laid in a line along the inside of a meander bend. Fairly uniform in profile and width, with mixed sediment sizes.

In what way do natural levees differ from point bars? (Levees: along both banks, coarse material from floods. Point bars: only on the inner side of bends, mixed sizes from flowing water.)

Exam tip. One line in the textbook places point bars on the "concave" side, but its meander section and Figure 6.7 put deposition on the convex (inner, slip-off) bank and erosion on the concave (outer, cut-off) bank. The animation follows the convex/concave rule.

Meanders and ox-bow lakes

On large flood and delta plains rivers rarely flow straight. Loop-like bends called meanders form. A meander is not a landform, only a type of channel pattern. It develops because (i) water on a very gentle gradient works sideways on its banks, (ii) loose alluvial banks have irregularities that the water can push against, and (iii) the Coriolis force deflects the flowing water.

02

Groundwater

Limestone and dolomite regions (rich in calcium carbonate)

Surface water sinks in easily where rocks are permeable, thinly bedded, jointed and cracked. It moves down, then sideways along bedding planes and joints. Mechanical removal by groundwater hardly matters. What matters is solution (dissolving) and precipitation (re-depositing), which only work strongly in limestone and dolomite.

A limestone region shaped by these two processes has karst topography, named after the Karst region of the Balkans next to the Adriatic Sea.

Erosional landforms

Swallow holes

Small to medium, round to sub-rounded shallow depressions formed on limestone by solution.

Sinkholes

Circular at the top, funnel-shaped below. From a few m² to a hectare in area; under half a metre to 30 m or more deep. Solution sinks are more common than collapse sinks (dolines). Soil-covered sinkholes can look like harmless pools.

Valley sinks (uvalas)

Long, narrow to wide trenches formed when sinkholes and dolines join through slumping of their margins or collapse of cave roofs.

Lapies

An extremely irregular surface of points, grooves and ridges, formed by differential solution along parallel joints. Lapie fields may smooth out into limestone pavements.

Caves and tunnels

Water dissolves limestone along bedding planes, opening long gaps. Common where limestone is thick and massive, or between beds of shale, sandstone or quartzite. A cave open at both ends is a tunnel.

Depositional landforms (inside caves)

How deposition happens

Calcium carbonate dissolves in carbonated rainwater. It is re-deposited when that water evaporates or loses its carbon dioxide while trickling over rock.

Stalactites

Hang from the roof like icicles; broad at the base, tapering to the free end.

Stalagmites

Rise from the cave floor where water drips from above or from a stalactite. May be columns, discs, rounded ends or small crater-like hollows.

Pillars / columns

A stalactite and stalagmite that grow until they fuse.

03

Glaciers

High mountains and high latitudes

Glaciers are masses of ice moving as sheets over land (continental glaciers, or piedmont glaciers spread over plains at the foot of mountains) or as linear flows down broad troughs (mountain and valley glaciers). They move slowly, a few centimetres to a few metres a day, because of gravity.

Their erosion is tremendous because of the sheer weight of the ice. Plucked angular blocks dragged along the floor and sides cause damage through abrasion and plucking, even on unweathered rock. Over time debris is removed, divides are lowered, and slopes become so gentle that the glacier stops, leaving low hills and vast outwash plains.

Cirques, horns and arêtes

Cirque

Most common glacial landform. A deep, long, wide basin at the head of a glacial valley with very steep, concave to vertical walls. Several can form a stepped sequence.

Tarn (cirque) lake

Lake left inside a cirque after the glacier melts.

Horn

High, sharp, steep peak formed when three or more radiating cirques cut headward until they meet. Matterhorn (Alps) and Everest (Himalaya) are horns.

Arête (serrated ridge)

The divide between cirque walls narrows into a saw-toothed ridge with a sharp crest and zig-zag outline.

From V to U: the glacial trough

Glacial valley / trough

U-shaped, with a broad floor and smooth, steep sides. May hold moraines, swamps, and lakes gouged from rock or dammed by debris.

Hanging valley

A tributary valley left high on the wall of the main glacial valley. Its spur faces are often cut into truncated spurs (triangular facets).

Fjord

A very deep glacial trough drowned by the sea, forming the coastline in high latitudes.

Glacial valley vs river valley: U-shape, broad flat floor and straight course vs V-shape, narrow floor and winding course with interlocking spurs.

Depositional landforms
ICE DEPOSIT

Glacial till

Unsorted mix of coarse and fine debris dropped by melting ice. Fragments are angular to sub-angular.

MELTWATER DEPOSIT

Outwash deposits

Glacio-fluvial material washed out by meltwater streams. Roughly stratified and sorted; fragments somewhat rounded.

Moraines

Long ridges of till. Terminal: at the end (toe). Lateral: along the sides, may join the terminal moraine in a horseshoe. Ground: an irregular sheet left by a retreating glacier. Medial: in the centre between laterals.

Esker

A sinuous ridge of boulders and gravel laid by a meltwater stream flowing in an ice channel beneath the glacier.

Outwash plain

Broad flat alluvial fans of glacio-fluvial gravel, silt, sand and clay that join at the foot of glacial mountains or beyond ice sheets.

Drumlin

Smooth oval ridge of till, up to about 1 km long and 30 m high, long axis parallel to ice flow. The blunt stoss end faces the glacier; the gentler end is the tail. Shows the direction of ice movement.

Glaciers in India. The Bhagirathi starts at Gaumukh, the snout of the Gangotri glacier. The Alkapuri glacier feeds the Alaknanda. The two rivers join at Devprayag to form the Ganga. Glaciers can be seen in the higher reaches of Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh and Jammu & Kashmir.
04

Waves and currents

Coasts — the most dynamic and destructive zone

Coastal change can be very fast: erosion in one season, deposition in the next. Breaking waves throw water hard against the shore and churn sediment on the sea floor. Storm and tsunami waves cause big changes in a short time. Coastal landforms also depend on the shape of land and sea floor, and on whether the coast is emerging (advancing seaward) or submerging (retreating landward).

High rocky coasts (submerged)

Drowned rivers, highly indented coastline, fjords where glacial valleys exist. Hills drop sharply into the sea. Erosional forms dominate. Example: India's west coast.

Low sedimentary coasts (emerged)

Rivers build coastal plains and deltas. Smooth coastline with lagoons and tidal creeks, marshes and swamps. Depositional forms dominate. Example: India's east coast.

Cliffs, caves and stacks

Wave-cut cliff

Steep sea cliff, a few metres to 30 m or more high, shaped by waves pounding the land.

Wave-cut platform / terrace

Flat or gently sloping rock surface left in front of the retreating cliff. Called a wave-cut terrace when it sits above average wave height.

Sea cave

Hollows at the cliff base, widened by waves and smashed debris. Roofs collapse and the cliff retreats.

Sea stack

Resistant rock left standing as a small island when the cliff retreats. Temporary; it too will be worn away.

Wave-built terrace

Broken, rounded cliff material deposited offshore, in front of the wave-cut terrace.

Bars, spits and lagoons

Beaches

Temporary deposits, mostly sand-sized, from rivers or wave erosion. Shingle beaches hold small pebbles and cobbles.

Sand dunes

Sand lifted from the beach and deposited just behind it as long ridges parallel to the coast. Common on low sedimentary coasts.

Off-shore bar

A ridge of sand and shingle in the offshore zone, roughly parallel to the coast. When it shows above water it is a barrier bar.

Spit

A barrier bar attached at one end to a headland of the bay. Example: Godavari delta.

Lagoon

A bay closed off by bars and spits. It fills with sediment and becomes a swamp, then a coastal plain.

Nature's coastal defence. Off-shore bars take the first blow from storms and tsunamis, followed by barriers, beaches, beach dunes and mangroves. Disturbing the sediment budget or cutting mangroves removes this buffer and leaves settlements exposed.
05

Winds

Hot deserts — wind and sheet floods are the two dominant agents

Dry, bare desert floors heat quickly and heat the air above, causing rising turbulent air, eddies, whirlwinds, updrafts and downdrafts. Wind works in three ways: deflation (lifting dust and fine particles), abrasion (sand grains scraping rock), and impact (the force of sand striking rock, like sand-blasting).

Wind is not the only agent. Rain is rare but torrential, and bare rocks weathered by big daily temperature swings are quickly washed off by sheet floods. Wind moves fine material; most mass erosion is by sheet wash. Desert stream channels are broad, smooth and short-lived.

Pediments and pediplains

Pediment

Gently inclined rocky floor at the foot of mountains, with or without thin debris. Made by lateral stream erosion plus sheet flooding.

Parallel retreat (backwasting)

The steep wash slope and free face above it retreat backwards at the same angle, so the pediment grows at the mountain's expense.

Inselberg

The last remnant of the worn-down mountain.

Pediplain

The low, nearly featureless plain that results.

Playa and alkali flat

In a basin ringed by hills, sediment fills the centre into a near-level plain. After rain it holds a shallow, short-lived lake (playa). When covered with salts it is an alkali flat.

Deflation hollows, blowouts, caves

Steady wind blows away loose material, leaving shallow hollows. Abrasion on rock faces makes blowouts, some deep enough to be caves.

Mushroom, table and pedestal rocks

Sand dunes

Wind sorts grains well: depending on its speed, grains roll, hop (saltation) or float in suspension, and settle by size when the wind dies. Dry hot deserts with a good supply of sand, a steady wind and obstacles to start piling are ideal. Arrows show wind direction.

Barchan

Crescent with horns pointing downwind. Constant, moderate wind over a uniform surface.

Parabolic

A reversed barchan, horns pointing upwind, held by vegetation on partly covered sand.

Seif

Like a barchan with only one wing, caused by a shift in wind. The lone wing can grow very long and high.

Transverse

Perpendicular to the wind. Steady wind with the sand source a long feature at right angles to it. Long and low.

Longitudinal

Long ridges parallel to the wind, low in height. Form when sand supply is poor and wind is constant.

When sand is plentiful, regular dunes merge and lose their shapes. Most desert dunes keep moving; some get fixed, especially near settlements.

Textbook exercise answers

2 (i)What do incised meanders in rocks and meanders in plains of alluvium indicate?

Meanders on alluvial plains show a very gentle gradient where the river works sideways on soft banks (old stage). Incised meanders cut deep into hard rock show a river that kept its winding course while cutting down vertically, a sign of renewed down-cutting, often after the land was uplifted.

2 (ii)Explain the evolution of valley sinks or uvalas.

Solution first makes swallow holes and sinkholes on limestone. When neighbouring sinkholes and dolines join, through slumping of their margins or collapse of cave roofs below, they form long, narrow to wide trenches called valley sinks or uvalas.

2 (iii)Why is underground flow more common than surface run-off in limestone areas?

Limestone is soluble and highly jointed. Rain enters swallow holes and sinkholes, widens joints and bedding planes by solution, and flows as underground streams, re-emerging downstream through cave openings.

2 (iv)Name the linear depositional forms in glacial valleys and give their locations.

Terminal moraine at the end (toe) of the glacier; lateral moraines along the valley sides; medial moraine in the centre between lateral moraines; eskers as winding ridges on the valley floor where sub-glacial streams flowed. Drumlins also lie on the floor, aligned with ice flow.

2 (v)How does wind do its work in deserts? Is it the only agent?

Wind erodes by deflation, abrasion and impact, and deposits sorted sand as dunes. It is not the only agent: torrential rain and sheet floods remove most weathered debris, and mass wasting helps. Pediments, for example, are made by streams and sheet floods.

3 (i)Running water is the most dominant agent in humid as well as arid climates. Explain.

In humid regions heavy rain feeds overland flow and rivers that cut rills, gullies and valleys, form potholes, gorges, canyons and terraces, and build fans, deltas, floodplains, levees and meanders. Over time they lower the land to a peneplain. In deserts rain is rare but torrential; on bare rock weathered by daily temperature changes, sheet floods and short-lived streams remove debris quickly. Pediments, pediplains, inselbergs and playas are all made largely by running water, so even in deserts it does more work than wind.

3 (ii)Why do limestones behave differently in humid and arid climates? What is the dominant process and its results?

Limestone dissolves in carbonated rainwater. In humid climates plenty of water causes vigorous solution, producing karst landforms. In arid climates there is little water, so solution is weak and limestone behaves like other rocks under mechanical weathering. The dominant, almost exclusive process is solution and precipitation. Results: swallow holes, sinkholes, dolines, uvalas, lapies, limestone pavements, caves and tunnels (erosional), and stalactites, stalagmites and pillars (depositional).

3 (iii)How do glaciers reduce high mountains into low hills and plains?

The heavy ice drags rock fragments that abrade and pluck the floor and walls. Cirques cut headward into peaks, leaving horns and arêtes; valleys are widened and deepened into U-shaped troughs. As debris is carried away, divides are lowered until slopes are too gentle for the ice to move. What remains is a mass of low hills with moraines, eskers, drumlins and broad outwash plains.

Composition and Structure of Atmosphere

The blanket of air we breathe, layer by layer

In short

  • The atmosphere is a mixture of gases, water vapour and dust particles. 99% of its mass lies within 32 km of the surface.
  • Carbon dioxide lets sunlight in but traps outgoing heat; ozone (10–50 km) filters ultraviolet rays; water vapour (0–4%) acts like a blanket.
  • Five layers by temperature: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere (ionosphere), exosphere.
  • All weather happens in the troposphere, about 8 km thick at the poles and 18 km at the equator.
01

Composition of the atmosphere

Gases, water vapour and dust

Air is colourless and odourless; we feel it only as wind. Humans can survive a while without food and water but not a few minutes without air. The mix changes with height: oxygen is almost negligible at 120 km, and carbon dioxide and water vapour are found only up to 90 km.

GAS

Carbon dioxide

Meteorologically very important: transparent to incoming solar radiation but opaque to outgoing terrestrial radiation. It absorbs some outgoing heat and sends some back to the surface, causing the greenhouse effect. Its volume is rising because of fossil fuel burning, which has also raised air temperature.

GAS

Ozone

Found 10–50 km above the surface. It acts as a filter, absorbing ultraviolet rays from the sun before they reach the surface.

VARIABLE

Water vapour

Decreases with height and from the equator to the poles. Up to 4% of air in warm, wet tropics; under 1% in dry deserts and polar areas. Absorbs part of the insolation and keeps the Earth's radiated heat, so the Earth is neither too hot nor too cold. Affects stability of the air.

SOLIDS

Solar Radiation, Heat Balance and Temperature

How the sun heats the Earth, and why the Earth neither warms up nor cools down overall

In short

  • The Earth receives insolation in short waves and radiates heat back in long waves. The atmosphere is heated mainly from below, by the Earth.
  • Insolation varies with the sun's angle (latitude), day length, Earth's tilt, atmospheric transparency and land aspect.
  • Out of 100 units arriving, 35 are reflected (albedo) and 65 absorbed; 65 units go back to space, so the heat budget balances.
  • Temperature depends on latitude, altitude, distance from sea, air masses and ocean currents. Normally it falls with height; when it rises with height, that is inversion.
01

Solar radiation

Incoming solar radiation = insolation

The Earth gets almost all its energy from the sun, mostly in short wavelengths. Because the Earth is a geoid, the sun's rays strike the top of the atmosphere obliquely, and the Earth intercepts only a tiny part of the sun's energy: on average 1.94 calories per cm² per minute at the top of the atmosphere.

Why insolation varies

(i) Rotation of the Earth on its axis · (ii) angle of the sun's rays · (iii) length of the day · (iv) transparency of the atmosphere · (v) configuration of land (aspect). The last two have less influence. The Earth's axis makes an angle of 66½° with its orbital plane, which strongly affects how much each latitude receives.

Angle of the sun's rays

Through the atmosphere

The atmosphere is largely transparent to short-wave radiation. In the troposphere, water vapour, ozone and other gases absorb much of the near-infrared. Tiny particles scatter visible light, giving the blue sky and red sunrises and sunsets.

Spatial distribution

About 320 W/m² in the tropics to about 70 W/m² at the poles. The subtropical deserts get the most (least cloud); the equator gets less than the tropics. At the same latitude, continents get more than oceans. Middle and high latitudes get less in winter.

02

Heating and cooling of the atmosphere

Conduction

Heat flows from a warmer body to a cooler one in contact until both are equally warm. Heats the lowest layers of air.

Convection

Heated air rises in currents and carries heat upward (vertical heating). Confined to the troposphere.

Advection

Heat carried by horizontal movement of air; more important than vertical movement. Causes most day-to-night changes in mid-latitudes. The loo of northern India in summer results from advection.

Terrestrial radiation

The heated Earth radiates long waves that greenhouse gases, especially carbon dioxide, absorb. So the atmosphere is heated indirectly, from below.

03

What comes in must go out

The Earth as a whole neither accumulates nor loses heat. That is possible only if the insolation received equals the heat lost by terrestrial radiation. Take insolation at the top of the atmosphere as 100 units and follow where it goes.

04

Temperature

Heat is the molecular movement of particles; temperature measures, in degrees, how hot or cold a thing or place is.

Latitude

Insolation varies with latitude, so temperature does too.

Altitude

The atmosphere is heated from below, so temperature generally falls with height. This normal lapse rate is 6.5°C per 1,000 m.

Distance from the sea

The sea heats and cools slowly; land does so quickly. Coastal places have smaller temperature variation, helped by land and sea breezes.

Air masses and ocean currents

Warm air masses and warm currents raise temperature; cold ones lower it.

Distribution of temperature

Key patterns

Isotherms join places of equal temperature and generally run parallel to latitudes. They deviate more in January and in the northern hemisphere, which has more land. Along 60°E, January temperature is −20°C at both 80°N and 50°N. January means: over 27°C in equatorial oceans, over 24°C in the tropics, 2° to 0°C in middle latitudes, −18° to −48°C in the Eurasian interior. In the southern hemisphere, the 20°, 10° and 0°C isotherms run along 35°S, 45°S and 60°S. In July, over 30°C occurs in subtropical Asia along 30°N. The annual range exceeds 60°C over north-eastern Eurasia (continentality) and is only about 3°C between 20°S and 15°N.

Inversion of temperature

Two laws. Planck's law: the hotter a body, the more energy it radiates and the shorter the wavelength. Specific heat: the energy needed to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by 1°C.

1 (iv)Match: insolation, albedo, isotherm, annual range

Insolation → the incoming solar radiation · Albedo → the percentage of visible light reflected by an object · Isotherm → lines joining places of equal temperature · Annual range → the difference between the mean temperature of the warmest and coldest months.

2 (i)How does unequal distribution of heat cause variations in weather and climate?

Unequal heating creates temperature differences, which create pressure differences. Air moves from high to low pressure as wind, carrying heat and moisture from one region to another. These movements, with the resulting clouds and rain, produce varied weather and climate.

2 (ii)What factors control temperature distribution on the surface?

Latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, air masses and ocean currents, and local aspects.

2 (iii)In India, why is day temperature highest in May and not after the summer solstice?

By May the sun is high over northern India, skies are clear and the land has been heating for weeks. After mid-June the monsoon brings clouds and rain, which cut insolation and cool the land, so temperatures fall despite the solstice.

2 (iv)Why is the annual range of temperature high in the Siberian plains?

Because of continentality: Siberia is far from the moderating influence of the sea. Land heats quickly in summer and cools greatly in winter, so the range exceeds 60°C over north-eastern Eurasia.

3 (i)How do latitude and the tilt of the Earth's axis affect radiation received?

The axis is inclined at 66½° to the orbital plane, so as the Earth revolves, the sun's vertical rays move between the tropics and day length changes with season and latitude. Higher latitudes receive the sun's rays at a lower angle. Slanting rays spread the same energy over a larger area, so energy per unit area falls. They also travel through a thicker layer of atmosphere, so more is absorbed, scattered and diffused. Low latitudes receive near-vertical rays all year and so get more insolation; high latitudes get little, especially in winter. That is why insolation is about 320 W/m² in the tropics and about 70 W/m² at the poles.

3 (ii)How does the earth–atmosphere system maintain heat balance?

Of 100 units of insolation at the top of the atmosphere, 35 are reflected back to space (27 from clouds, 2 from snow and ice, the rest scattered): the Earth's albedo. Of the remaining 65, 14 are absorbed by the atmosphere and 51 by the surface. The surface radiates 51 units: 17 go straight to space and 34 are absorbed by the atmosphere (6 directly, 9 through convection and turbulence, 19 through latent heat of condensation). The atmosphere then radiates its 48 units (14 + 34) to space. Total outgoing is 17 + 48 = 65 units, equal to the 65 absorbed. So the Earth neither warms up nor cools down overall. Latitudinal surplus between 40°N and 40°S and deficit near the poles are balanced by poleward transfer of heat by winds and ocean currents.

3 (iii)Compare January temperature distribution in the two hemispheres.

In the northern hemisphere, with more land, isotherms deviate strongly from latitudes in January: they bend north over oceans (warmed by the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift) and south over continents, especially over Europe and the Siberian plain. Temperatures fall very low in the continental interior (−18° to −48°C in Eurasia), and along 60°E it is −20°C at both 80°N and 50°N. In the southern hemisphere, dominated by ocean, isotherms run more or less parallel to latitudes and temperature changes gradually: the 20°, 10° and 0°C isotherms follow 35°S, 45°S and 60°S. So land–sea distribution makes the northern pattern irregular and extreme and the southern one regular and moderate.

Atmospheric Circulation and Weather Systems

Why the wind blows, where it blows, and how storms are born

In short

  • Uneven heating makes pressure differences; air moves from high to low pressure as wind.
  • Wind is shaped by three forces: the pressure gradient force, friction and the Coriolis force (right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern; zero at the equator).
  • Pressure belts and the three cells (Hadley, Ferrel, polar) produce the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies. Belts shift with the sun.
  • Air masses meet at fronts, forming extra-tropical cyclones. Tropical cyclones draw energy from warm seas (over 27°C) and die over land.

Atmospheric pressure

Measured in millibars with mercury or aneroid barometers

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of a column of air on a unit area from sea level to the top of the atmosphere. Average sea-level pressure is 1,013.2 mb. Air at the surface is denser, so pressure is highest there.

In the lower atmosphere pressure falls by about 1 mb for every 10 m of height, though not always at the same rate. The vertical pressure gradient force is much larger than the horizontal one, but gravity nearly balances it, so we do not get strong upward winds.

02

Forces that steer the wind

Pressure gradient force

The rate of change of pressure with distance. Strong where isobars are close together, weak where they are far apart. Acts perpendicular to isobars.

Frictional force

Slows the wind. Greatest at the surface, felt up to 1–3 km; minimal over the sea.

Coriolis force

From the Earth's rotation (described in 1844). Deflects wind to the right in the northern and left in the southern hemisphere. Stronger for faster winds; proportional to latitude: maximum at the poles, zero at the equator.

2–3 km up, free of friction, where isobars are straight, the pressure gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force and the wind blows parallel to the isobars.

03

General circulation of the atmosphere

The pattern of planetary winds depends on (i) latitudinal variation of heating, (ii) pressure belts, (iii) migration of belts with the sun, (iv) distribution of continents and oceans and (v) the Earth's rotation. This circulation also sets ocean currents moving, and the oceans in turn feed energy and water vapour back to the air.

Why subtropical highs form at 30°

Air rising at the ITCZ (to about 14 km) flows poleward aloft and accumulates around 30°N and S. Part of it sinks, also because it has cooled, creating high pressure at the surface.

El Niño and ENSO

Warm water from the central Pacific drifts to the South American coast and replaces the cold Peruvian current: El Niño. The linked pressure change over the central Pacific and Australia is the Southern Oscillation. Together they are ENSO. In strong ENSO years the arid west coast of South America gets heavy rain, Australia and sometimes India suffer drought, and China gets floods. It is used for long-range forecasting.

Seasonal and local winds

The shifting of heating, pressure and wind belts with the seasons modifies circulation; the most pronounced example is the monsoon of south-east Asia. Daily heating cycles also create local winds.

Katabatic wind

Cold air from high plateaus and ice fields draining down into valleys.

Warm leeward wind

Moist wind rises over a range, condenses and rains on the windward side, then descends the leeward slope as dry air warmed adiabatically. It can melt snow in a short time.

Tropical cyclones

Violent storms born over warm tropical oceans

They bring violent winds, very heavy rain and storm surges that flood coastal lowlands. Names: cyclones in the Indian Ocean, hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the western Pacific and South China Sea, willy-willies in western Australia.

Conditions needed

3 (i)Discuss the factors affecting the speed and direction of wind.

Wind blows from high to low pressure and is controlled by three forces plus gravity. The pressure gradient force (rate of change of pressure with distance) drives the wind; it is strong where isobars are close, giving faster winds, and acts perpendicular to isobars. Water in the Atmosphere

From invisible vapour to dew, fog, clouds and rain

In short

  • Air holds 0–4% water vapour (humidity). Warm air can hold more than cold air.
  • Relative humidity = moisture present compared with full capacity at that temperature. Air at 100% is saturated; the temperature where this happens is the dew point.
  • Cooling below the dew point causes condensation: dew, frost, fog and mist, and clouds (cirrus, cumulus, stratus, nimbus).
  • Precipitation (rain, snow, sleet, hail) comes as convectional, orographic or cyclonic rain. Rainfall generally decreases from the equator to the poles.
01

Humidity

Water vapour present in the air

Water exists in the atmosphere as gas, liquid and solid. Moisture comes from water bodies by evaporation and from plants by transpiration, and is constantly exchanged between atmosphere, oceans and continents through evaporation, transpiration, condensation and precipitation.

Absolute humidity

The actual amount of water vapour: weight of vapour per unit volume of air, in grams per cubic metre. Varies from place to place.

Relative humidity

The percentage of moisture present compared with the air's full capacity at that temperature. Changes as temperature changes. Greater over oceans, least over continents.

Saturated air

Air holding moisture to its full capacity at a given temperature; it cannot hold any more.

Dew point

The temperature at which a given sample of air becomes saturated.

02

Evaporation and condensation

Evaporation: liquid → vapour

Caused mainly by heat. Latent heat of vaporisation is the heat needed to turn a unit mass of liquid into vapour without changing its temperature. Higher temperature raises the air's capacity to absorb moisture; dry air can take more; and moving air replaces saturated air with unsaturated air, so more wind means more evaporation.

Condensation: vapour → water

Caused by loss of heat. In free air it happens around tiny hygroscopic condensation nuclei: dust, smoke and sea salt, which absorb water. It also happens when moist air touches a colder object. It occurs when air is cooled to its dew point at constant volume, when volume and temperature both fall, or when moisture is added by evaporation. The most favourable condition is a fall in air temperature.

Forms of condensation

Dew

Water droplets on cool solid surfaces (stones, grass, leaves). Needs a clear sky, calm air, high relative humidity and cold, long nights, with the dew point above freezing.

Frost

Forms when the dew point is at or below 0°C: moisture is deposited as minute ice crystals. Same ideal conditions as dew, with air at or below freezing.

Fog

A cloud with its base at or near the ground, formed when moist air cools suddenly. Visibility under 1 km. Common where warm and cold air currents meet. Fog mixed with smoke is smog.

Mist

Contains more moisture than fog (each nucleus has a thicker layer of water). Visibility 1–2 km. Frequent over mountains where rising warm air meets cold surfaces.

03

Clouds

Minute water droplets or ice crystals formed in free air at height
04

Precipitation

Continued condensation makes droplets grow until air resistance can no longer hold them up, and they fall. Water falling is rainfall; below 0°C it falls as snowfall, as hexagonal crystals that form flakes.

Sleet

Frozen raindrops or refrozen melted snow. Forms when a layer of air above freezing lies over a sub-freezing layer near the ground; drops reach the ground as small ice pellets no bigger than raindrops.

Hail

Raindrops that pass through colder layers and solidify into rounded pieces of ice with several concentric layers.

Types of rainfall

World distribution of rainfall

General patterns

Rainfall decreases steadily from the equator to the poles. Coasts get more than continental interiors, and oceans more than land. Between 35° and 40° N and S, rain is heavier on eastern coasts and decreases westward. Between 45° and 65° N and S, the westerlies bring rain first to western margins, decreasing eastward. Where mountains run parallel to a coast, the windward coastal plain gets more.

Precipitation regimes

Over 200 cm: equatorial belt, windward western coasts of the cool temperate zone, coasts of monsoon lands. 100–200 cm: interior continental areas; coastal areas moderate. 50–100 cm: central tropical lands, eastern and interior temperate lands. Under 50 cm: rain-shadow interiors and high latitudes. Seasonal spread matters: the equatorial belt and western cool temperate areas get rain all year.

It needs a clear sky, calm air, high relative humidity and cold, long nights, with the dew point above freezing. Frost forms on cold surfaces when condensation happens below freezing, i.e. the dew point is at or below 0°C, so the excess moisture is deposited as minute ice crystals instead of droplets. Its ideal conditions are the same as for dew, except that the air temperature must be at or below freezing.

World Climate and Climate Change

Sorting the world's climates with letters, and why the climate keeps changing

In short

  • Climate can be classified empirically (observed data), genetically (by causes) or for applied purposes.

02

Climate change

A natural and continuous process, now speeded up by humans

Today's climate has prevailed for about 10,000 years with small and occasionally large fluctuations. Evidence of past change comes from glacial landforms, sediments in glacial lakes, tree rings (wet and dry periods) and historical records.

  1. 500–300 million years ago

    The Earth was warm through the Cambrian, Ordovician and Silurian periods.

  2. Pleistocene · peak 18,000 years ago

    Glacial and interglacial periods; the last major glacial peak about 18,000 years ago. The present interglacial began 10,000 years ago.

  3. 8,000 BC · 3,000–1,700 BC

    The Rajasthan desert was wet and cool around 8,000 BC; rainfall was higher 3,000–1,700 BC; the Harappan civilisation flourished there 2,000–1,700 BC. Dry conditions have increased since.

  4. 10th–11th centuries

    Warm, dry conditions in Europe; the Vikings settled in Greenland.

  5. 1550 – about 1850

    Europe's "Little Ice Age".

  6. 1885–1940

    World temperature trended upward; after 1940 the rise slowed.

  7. 1930s · 1967–77 · 1990s

    The "dust bowl" drought on the south-western Great Plains of the USA; the devastating Sahel drought south of the Sahara; the 1990s had the century's warmest temperatures and some of the worst floods.

Causes

ASTRONOMICAL

Sunspots

Dark, cooler patches on the sun that rise and fall in cycles. Some meteorologists link more sunspots to cooler, wetter, stormier weather and fewer to warm, dry conditions, but the findings are not statistically significant.

ASTRONOMICAL

Milankovitch oscillations

Cycles in the Earth's orbit, its wobble and its axial tilt that change the insolation received.

TERRESTRIAL

Volcanism

Eruptions throw aerosols into the air that reduce sunlight reaching the surface for a long time. After Pinatubo and El Chichón, average temperatures fell somewhat for some years.

ANTHROPOGENIC

Greenhouse gases

The most important human effect: rising concentrations of greenhouse gases, likely to cause global warming.

03

Global warming

The atmosphere lets in short-wave solar radiation but absorbs most of the long-wave radiation emitted by the surface, like the glass of a greenhouse. You feel the same effect in a closed car in summer. Gases that absorb long-wave radiation are greenhouse gases (GHGs); the warming they cause is the greenhouse effect.

The main GHGs

Carbon dioxide (CO₂), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and ozone (O₃). Nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide (CO) react with GHGs and affect their concentration.

What makes a GHG effective

How much its concentration rises, how long it stays, and which wavelengths it absorbs. CFCs are highly effective. Ozone absorbs UV in the stratosphere but traps terrestrial radiation when in the lower troposphere. Longer-lived gases take longer to recover from.

Carbon dioxide

The largest GHG concentration, mainly from burning oil, gas and coal. Forests and oceans are sinks; deforestation adds CO₂. It takes 20–50 years to adjust to changes, and rises about 0.5% a year. Doubling over pre-industrial levels is used as an index in climate models.

Kyoto Protocol

Proclaimed in 1997, in force from 2005, ratified by 141 nations. It bound 35 industrialised countries to cut emissions by 2012 to 5% below 1990 levels.

The temperature record

The world's average near-surface temperature is about 14°C. The 20th century warmed most in 1901–44 and 1977–99, about 0.4°C in each, with slight cooling in between. By 2000 the global mean was about 0.6°C above the end of the 19th century. The seven warmest years of 1856–2000 were in the last decade, and 1998 was the warmest.

Consequences. Warming may be uneven but will harm life-support systems. Melting glaciers and ice caps and thermal expansion of seawater raise sea level, which could flood coastal areas and islands. Once warming sets in, it is hard to reverse.

Water (Oceans)

The water cycle, the hidden landscape of the ocean floor, and how temperature and salt vary in the sea

In short

  • Water moves endlessly between oceans, air, land and living things: the hydrological cycle. Renewable water is constant, but demand keeps rising.

01

The hydrological cycle

Earth is the "Blue Planet"

Water is a cyclic resource that can be used and reused. The hydrological cycle is the circulation of water within the hydrosphere in liquid, solid and gaseous forms, and its continuous exchange between oceans, atmosphere, land surface, subsurface and organisms. It has worked for billions of years and all life depends on it. About 91% of the planet's water is in the oceans (NCERT figure); the rest is freshwater in glaciers and ice caps, groundwater, lakes, soil moisture, the atmosphere, streams and living things.

03

Temperature of ocean waters

Oceans heat and cool more slowly than land. The surface is always warmest because it receives the sun's heat directly; heat moves down by convection. Temperature falls quickly to about 200 m and then more slowly.

Latitude

Surface temperature falls from the equator to the poles as insolation decreases.

Land and water

Northern hemisphere oceans touch more land and receive more heat than southern ones.

Prevailing wind

Offshore winds drive warm surface water away, causing upwelling of cold water; onshore winds pile warm water up near the coast.

Ocean currents

Warm currents (Gulf Stream) warm the east coast of North America and west coast of Europe; cold currents (Labrador) cool the north-east coast of North America.

Enclosed seas in low latitudes are warmer than open seas; enclosed seas in high latitudes are colder than open seas.

Surface temperature facts. Average surface temperature is about 27°C, falling about 0.5°C per degree of latitude: about 22°C at 20°, 14°C at 40°, 0°C near the poles. The warmest water is slightly north of the equator. Northern hemisphere oceans average about 19°C, southern about 16°C, because of unequal land and water.

2 (i)Why do we call the Earth the Blue Planet?

Because it has an abundant supply of water on its surface, mostly in oceans, which makes it look blue from space. Water is rare elsewhere in the solar system.

The transition zone between the continental shore and the deep-sea basin, including the continental shelf, slope, rise and deep-oceanic trenches.

The boundary zone below the warm surface layer, starting about 100–400 m down and extending several hundred metres, where temperature decreases rapidly with depth.

2 (v)What thermal layers would you meet going down into the ocean, and why?

In middle and low latitudes: a warm surface layer (about 500 m, 20–25°C), the thermocline (500–1,000 m thick) with a rapid fall, and a very cold deep layer down to the floor. Temperature falls with depth because the sun heats only the surface and heat moves down slowly by convection. Near the poles there is one cold layer from surface to floor.

2 (vi)What is salinity of sea water?

The total content of dissolved salts, measured as grams of salt per 1,000 g (1 kg) of sea water and expressed in parts per thousand (‰).

3 (i)How are the elements of the hydrological cycle interrelated?

Water evaporates from oceans and other water bodies, plants add vapour by transpiration, and ice sublimates. The vapour rises, cools and condenses into clouds, then falls as precipitation. On land, part is stored as ice and snow and later melts into streams; part runs off as surface water in rivers and lakes; part infiltrates into the soil and becomes groundwater, which discharges into rivers, springs and the sea. Rivers carry water back to the oceans, completing the cycle. Each stage feeds the next: more evaporation means more precipitation, and more infiltration means more groundwater and steadier river flow. The total renewable water stays constant, so pollution or overuse at one stage affects all the others.

3 (ii)Examine the factors that influence ocean temperature distribution.

(1) Latitude: insolation falls poleward, so surface temperature drops about 0.5°C per degree of latitude, from about 27°C average near the equator to 0°C near the poles. (2) Unequal land and water: northern hemisphere oceans touch more land and are warmer (about 19°C) than southern ones (about 16°C), and the warmest water lies slightly north of the equator. (3) Prevailing winds: offshore winds drive warm surface water away and cause upwelling of cold water; onshore winds pile up warm water. (4) Ocean currents: warm currents such as the Gulf Stream raise temperatures; cold currents such as the Labrador Current lower them. (5) Enclosed seas are warmer than open seas in low latitudes and colder in high latitudes. Vertically, temperature falls with depth through the surface layer, thermocline and cold deep layer.

Movements of Ocean Water

Waves, tides and currents: how and why the sea moves

In short

  • Waves are energy moving across the surface; the water itself only moves in small circles. Most are made by wind.
  • Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day, caused by the pull of the moon (mainly), the sun and the centrifugal force. Spring tides come at new and full moon, neap tides about 7 days later.
  • Currents are river-like flows in a definite direction, driven by solar heating, wind, gravity and the Coriolis force, forming circular gyres.
  • Warm currents flow along east coasts and cold currents along west coasts in low and middle latitudes; where they meet are the best fishing grounds.
01

Ocean water is dynamic

Temperature, salinity and density, together with forces from the sun, moon and wind, move ocean water. Horizontal motions are currents and waves; vertical motions are tides, plus upwelling of cold water and sinking of surface water. In currents, water actually travels from place to place; in waves the water stays, and only the wave train moves ahead.

02

Waves

How waves form and travel

Wind blows over water: a breeze of 2 knots or less makes ripples, which grow until white caps appear. Wind pushes the water while gravity pulls crests down; falling water pushes former troughs up. Waves can travel thousands of km before breaking as surf.

Reading a wave

Steep waves are young, made by local wind. Slow, steady waves come from far away, perhaps another hemisphere. Maximum height depends on wind strength, how long it blows, and the area over which it blows in one direction.

Breaking

Near the beach, friction with the sea floor slows the wave. When the water depth is less than half the wavelength, the wave breaks. The largest waves are in the open ocean.

Tides

Periodic rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day

The moon's gravity (mostly), the sun's gravity (less) and the centrifugal force create two tidal bulges. On the side facing the moon, the moon's pull is greater than the centrifugal force, so water bulges towards the moon. On the far side the pull is weaker and the centrifugal force dominates, so water bulges away from the moon. The tide-generating force is the difference between the two; horizontal components matter more than vertical ones. Water movements caused by wind and pressure changes are surges, which are irregular.

Tides can be predicted well in advance, helping navigators and fishermen. High tide lets ships cross shallow "bars" at harbour entrances near rivers and estuaries. Ocean currents

Like rivers flowing in the ocean

Primary forces (start movement)

(i) Solar heating makes water expand, so near the equator the sea is about 8 cm higher than in middle latitudes; water flows down this slight slope. (ii) Wind pushes the surface water by friction. (iii) Gravity pulls water down the pile. (iv) The Coriolis force turns it right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern. The resulting circular flows are gyres.

Characteristics

Speed is called drift, measured in knots. Currents are strongest at the surface (over 5 knots) and slow at depth (under 0.5 knots). Denser water (saltier or colder) sinks: cold water sinks at the poles and moves slowly towards the equator, while warm surface water flows poleward to replace it.

BY DEPTH

The other 90%. Move around ocean basins through density and gravity; sink into deep basins at high latitudes where cold raises density.

BY TEMPERATURE

Mostly from wind blowing against the water surface. The energy is released on shorelines when waves break.

The periodic rise and fall of sea level, once or twice a day, mainly due to the attraction of the sun and the moon.

2 (iv)How are tides caused?

By the moon's gravitational pull (mostly), the sun's pull (less) and the centrifugal force. The moon's pull exceeds the centrifugal force on the near side, creating one bulge; the centrifugal force dominates on the far side, creating a second bulge.

2 (v)How are tides related to navigation?

Currents carry heat between latitudes. In the North Atlantic, the warm Gulf Stream and its continuation, the North Atlantic Drift, flow north-east to the west coast of Europe. They keep north-western Europe much warmer in winter than other places at the same latitude, give it cool summers and mild winters with a narrow annual range (marine west coast climate), keep its ports ice-free, and push isotherms northwards over the ocean in January.

Density differences from salinity and temperature cause vertical movement: cold, salty water sinks at high latitudes and moves slowly towards the equator, while warm surface water flows poleward. Biodiversity and Conservation

The variety of life, why it matters, and how we can protect it

In short

  • Biodiversity ("bio" = life, "diversity" = variety) is the number and variety of organisms in a region: plants, animals, micro-organisms, their genes and their ecosystems.
  • It works at three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem. It is richest in the tropics and poorest near the poles.
  • It plays ecological, economic and scientific roles. Diverse ecosystems are more stable.
  • It is being lost to habitat destruction, over-exploitation, pollution, exotic species, poaching and natural calamities. Conservation includes the Rio Earth Summit (1992), India's Wild Life (Protection) Act (1972), mega diversity centres and hotspots.
01

What is biodiversity?

The weathering mantle (Chapter 5) is the base for the diversity of vegetation and hence biodiversity. The underlying cause is the input of solar energy and water: regions rich in both have the widest range of life.

Today's biodiversity is the result of 2.5–3.5 billion years of evolution. Before humans arrived, the Earth supported more biodiversity than at any other time; since then it has declined rapidly. Estimates of the number of species range from 2 million to 100 million, with 10 million the best estimate; many are still unclassified (about 40% of South American freshwater fishes). The average half-life of a species is 1–4 million years, and 99% of all species that ever lived are now extinct.

LEVEL 1

Genetic diversity

Variation of genes within a species. Humans all belong to Homo sapiens yet differ in height, colour and appearance because of genetic diversity, which is essential for healthy breeding.

LEVEL 2

Species diversity

The variety and number of species in an area, measured by richness, abundance and types. Areas rich in species are hotspots.

LEVEL 3

Ecosystem diversity

Differences between ecosystem types and the variety of habitats and ecological processes within them. Ecosystem boundaries are not rigid. Example: grasslands and sholas of Indira Gandhi National Park, Annamalai, Western Ghats.

Richer in the tropics

Biodiversity is not spread evenly. It is consistently richer in the tropics; towards the poles there are larger and larger populations of fewer and fewer species. Slide from the equator to the pole.

02

Importance of biodiversity

Ecological role

Every organism takes what it needs and also contributes something useful to others. Species capture and store energy, produce and decompose organic matter, cycle water and nutrients, fix atmospheric gases and help regulate climate. The more diverse an ecosystem, the better it survives adversity, the more productive and the more stable it is.

Economic role

A resource for daily life: food crops (crop diversity or agro-biodiversity), livestock, forests, fish and medicines, and raw materials for food, pharmaceuticals and cosmetics. Treating it only as a resource has also caused its decline and conflicts over sharing it.

Scientific role

Each species offers clues to how life evolved and will evolve, and to how ecosystems work. Ethically, every species has an intrinsic right to exist; it is morally wrong to knowingly cause an extinction.

03

Loss of biodiversity

Population growth has increased consumption of natural resources and sped up the loss of species and habitats. The tropics cover only about one-fourth of the world's area but hold about three-fourths of its people, and tropical rain forests contain about 50% of the species on Earth, so their destruction is disastrous for the whole biosphere.

Habitat destruction

Over-exploitation of resources and deforestation to meet the needs of large populations.

Natural calamities

Earthquakes, floods, volcanic eruptions, forest fires and droughts damage flora and fauna.

Pollution

Pesticides, hydrocarbons and toxic heavy metals destroy weak and sensitive species.

Exotic species

Species introduced into a habitat where they are not natural inhabitants; they have often badly damaged natural communities.

Poaching

Tigers, elephants, rhinoceros, crocodiles, minks and birds hunted for horns, tusks and hides, pushing some into the endangered category.

04

Conservation of biodiversity

All life is interlinked: disturbing one part unbalances others and may threaten human existence. People need to adopt environment-friendly practices so development is sustainable. Conservation with sustainable use works only with the involvement of local communities and local institutions; the real challenge is to keep the process of conservation going.

World Conservation Strategy

India and 155 other nations signed the Convention on Biodiversity at the Earth Summit, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, June 1992. Suggested steps: (i) preserve endangered species; (ii) plan and manage to prevent extinction; (iii) preserve varieties of food crops, forage plants, timber trees, livestock and their wild relatives; (iv) identify and protect habitats of wild relatives; (v) protect habitats where species feed, breed, rest and nurse young; (vi) regulate international trade in wild plants and animals.

India

The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 set up national parks and sanctuaries and declared biosphere reserves to protect, preserve and propagate species within natural boundaries.

Mega diversity centres

12 tropical countries with a large share of the world's species: Mexico, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Brazil, Democratic Republic of Congo, Madagascar, China, India, Malaysia, Indonesia and Australia.

Biodiversity hotspots

Areas identified by IUCN to focus resources where species are most vulnerable. Defined by their vegetation, because plants determine primary productivity. In Madagascar about 85% of plants and animals are found nowhere else. Hawaii's unique species are threatened by introduced species and land development.

Exercise answers

2 (i)What is biodiversity?

The number and variety of organisms in a given region: plants, animals and micro-organisms, the genes they contain and the ecosystems they form, including variability within and between species and ecosystems.

2 (ii)What are the levels of biodiversity?

Genetic diversity (variation of genes within a species), species diversity (variety and number of species in an area) and ecosystem diversity (variety of ecosystem types, habitats and processes).

2 (iii)What are "hotspots"?

Areas very rich in species diversity, identified by IUCN on the basis of their vegetation, where conservation effort is concentrated because the species there are most vulnerable. Madagascar is an example.

2 (iv)Discuss briefly the importance of animals to humankind.

Animals give us food (livestock, fish), materials (wool, leather), medicines and labour. They pollinate crops, disperse seeds, decompose matter and keep ecosystems balanced. They help science understand evolution, and many are part of human culture.

2 (v)What are "exotic species"?

Species that are not natural inhabitants of a local habitat but are introduced into it. They have often caused extensive damage to the natural community of the ecosystem.

3 (i)What roles does biodiversity play in shaping nature?

Ecological: every species performs functions that sustain the ecosystem: capturing and storing energy, producing and decomposing organic matter, cycling water and nutrients, fixing atmospheric gases and helping regulate climate. Greater diversity makes an ecosystem more productive, better able to survive adversity and adapt to change, and more stable; losing species weakens its ability to maintain itself. Economic: biodiversity supplies food crops (agro-biodiversity), livestock, forests, fish and medicinal resources, and raw materials for food, pharmaceuticals and cosmetics. Scientific: each species gives clues to how life evolved and how ecosystems function. Ethically, every species has an intrinsic right to exist, and the level of biodiversity indicates the health of our relationship with other species.

3 (ii)What causes loss of biodiversity, and what steps can prevent it?

Causes: rapid population growth and over-consumption of resources; deforestation and destruction of tropical rain forests, which hold about half of all species; natural calamities such as earthquakes, floods, volcanic eruptions, forest fires and droughts; pollution by pesticides, hydrocarbons and toxic heavy metals; introduction of exotic species; and poaching of tigers, elephants, rhinos, crocodiles, minks and birds. Steps: preserve endangered species and plan to prevent extinction; preserve varieties of crops, forage plants, timber trees, livestock and their wild relatives; identify and protect habitats where species feed, breed, rest and nurse their young; regulate international trade in wildlife; set up national parks, sanctuaries and biosphere reserves under laws such as India's Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972; focus on hotspots and mega diversity centres; follow international agreements like the 1992 Convention on Biodiversity; and involve local communities and educate people in environment-friendly, sustainable practices.
